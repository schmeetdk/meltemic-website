01 The lens

What Peters actually asks of a logo

Allan Peters is Partner and Chief Creative Officer at Peters Design Company, and has worked on identity for Nike, Amazon, Target and Patagonia. His book Logos That Last is the clearest statement of his method, and it is unusually specific — which makes it a usable review instrument rather than a set of adjectives.

Three of his ideas do most of the work here.

Permanence-first. Longevity is not a byproduct of good design, it is a design objective in itself. A logo that feels current this year will feel dated in five; one built on the brand's actual truth has indefinite forward momentum.

Three levels, simultaneously. Conceptual clarity — built on what the brand operationally is, not what it aspires to. Formal integrity — curve geometry, stroke weight and optical spacing, judged by whether the mark still reads at 16 px. System expandability — a logo is a seed, and the question is what it grows into.

The practical tests. You need the icon alone at 72 × 72 for avatars and app tiles — typography-only logos become unreadable when miniaturized. Pressure-test early: wrong color, wrong size, hostile surroundings. And the one everybody quotes: would you be proud to wear it on a hat?

Measured against that, the old Meltemic logo has one real problem and seven consequences of it.

02 The audit

What was there

  1. 01

    There is no mark, so there is nothing to put anywhere

    No favicon, no LinkedIn avatar, no app tile, no Open Graph image. This is the failure Peters names most bluntly: you need the icon on its own at 72 × 72, and a wordmark cannot do that job.

    ↳ formal integrity · the 72×72 test
  2. 02

    The angle bracket is the most-used signifier in the category

    “< means we write code” is the default move for every dev shop and technical consultancy. It fails unique outright. It is also an unclosed bracket, which to the audience most likely to notice reads as a syntax error, and it hangs a fragment off the left edge where the lockup should have a clean vertical.

    ↳ simple · unique · memorable
  3. 03

    The logo was rendering in a fallback font

    The stylesheet asked for 'Biome' — a licensed Monotype face that was never loaded — and fell back to Orbitron. Anyone without Biome installed locally, which is everyone, saw a font nobody chose. The brand's single most important asset was rendering by accident.

    ↳ formal integrity
  4. 04

    Orbitron is a dated trend face

    Orbitron reads as 2010s sci-fi UI. Peters' test is whether a mark could have been drawn fifty years ago and still look current; this one is pinned to a decade.

    ↳ permanence-first
  5. 05

    The name is the strongest asset and the mark ignored it

    Meltemic is named for the Meltemi. The logo said nothing about wind, water, Greece or the sea — it substituted a generic tech signal for a specific, ownable, personally earned story. This is the finding everything else follows from.

    ↳ conceptual clarity · operational truth
  6. 06

    The color was the framework default

    #2563EB is Tailwind blue-600, unmodified, on slate grays. Blue is the most crowded color in B2B software, and this is its most crowded shade.

    ↳ simple · unique · memorable
  7. 07

    Emoji were doing the work of iconography

    🚀 📈 🤖 are not brand assets. They render differently on every operating system, carry someone else's drawing style, and cannot be extended.

    ↳ system expandability
  8. 08

    Nothing could grow from it

    No pattern, no icon language, no secondary device, no rules. There was a logotype and then a stock Bootstrap-flavoured page around it.

    ↳ system expandability
03 The concept

The name was already doing the work

The Meltemi is the summer wind of the Aegean. The Greeks called it the Etesian — from etesios, “annual”. It arrives every year, from the same quarter, with roughly the same force, between June and September. It is not a storm. It is weather you plan around.

That is an unusually good fit for a firm selling strategic operations. Markets, capital and AI are exactly that kind of weather: strong, constant, and not yours to control. You don't fight the Meltemi — you set a course that uses it.

And the founder isn't borrowing the metaphor. He is a Dane who learned the water in Scandinavian seas — a lifetime of sailing and kitesurfing in the cold north — and who now lives in Tel Aviv, on the Mediterranean. He added the c to Meltemi himself, to give the name a technical edge.

Peters asks for conceptual clarity built on operational truth rather than aspiration. This name is not a holiday memory. It marks a move — from northern water to a southern summer wind — made by someone who has spent his life reading both. The identity that follows says the same thing twice: Nordic restraint in the typography, Aegean weather in the mark.

Which produces the form. A boat cannot sail directly into the wind; it beats upwind in a zigzag, tacking. Draw that course on a chart and you draw a pointed M — the monogram, the wave and the tactic in one shape.

05 The mark

Drawn to a specification, not by eye

A monoline M, splayed wide and raked 7° to starboard, with every terminal cut flat. It reads as the letter, as a wave, and as a tacking course. The two counters are sails.

Plate 3 The mark at full size. Every terminal — the two feet and both apexes — is a horizontal cut. That single rule is what stops it reading as a generic lightning zigzag.
Parameter Value Why
Stroke, perpendicular 19 units Matched to Archivo 700's stem, then over-weighted — diagonals read lighter than verticals
Splay, horizontal step 28 units Wide enough to read as a wave; narrower and it becomes a spike
Centreline height 76 units Sets the 20° stroke angle — the system's Meltemi angle
Middle vertex −14 units Lifted above the feet so the M doesn't split into two chevrons at small sizes
Apex mitred, cut 12 A razor point disappears below 16 px; a flat apex survives
Feet flat, on baseline The mark sits on a line, so it never looks sheared
Lean 7° Enough to read as motion at 15 px, not enough to read as italic

Why generated rather than drawn. Every curve here comes from a parametric script, so the mark can be rebuilt exactly, at any weight, forever. The build files ship in brand/src/. Peters' formal integrity is easier to keep when the geometry is a specification rather than a memory.

06 The wordmark

Lowercase, and one custom letter

Archivo, weight 700, tracked −26/1000 em, all lowercase. Archivo is a sturdy grotesque drawn from 19th-century American models — it has backbone without being fashionable, which is what permanence-first asks for.

Keeping it lowercase is not a startup affectation. It is kleinschreibung, the Bauhaus convention — which is also the modernism of Tel Aviv's White City, and the restraint of Danish design. For this founder it is the historically correct register, and it was already his instinct.

Then one letter is customized: the final c. Its aperture is re-cut as a wedge at ±18°, opening both terminals on an angle that rhymes with the mark's apex. The c is the letter that turned Meltemi into a company, so it is the letter that carries the craft.

07 Colour

Limestone, ink and one clay accent

Tailwind blue is gone. The palette comes from the same place as the name: warm limestone paper, deep Aegean ink, and a single Cycladic terracotta reserved for calls to action. Every pairing was measured rather than eyeballed.

09 Next

Three things I'd do next

  • Put the founder story on the site properly. A Dane who learned the sea in Scandinavian water and now runs a strategy firm from Tel Aviv, named for the Mediterranean wind he moved into, is far more memorable than “veteran executive with a deep background”. The About page is currently the weakest, most generic thing on the site and it is sitting on the best material.
  • Take the identity to LinkedIn. The mark exists now specifically so that linkedin.com/in/peter-bondrop and the Meltemic company page can carry it. Right now the brand stops at the website.
  • Decide on the clay. Warm limestone with a terracotta accent is a genuinely common look right now. It's the right answer here because it comes from Cycladic architecture rather than from a mood board — but if it starts to feel familiar, the alternative is an ink-dominant site with limestone as the accent and clay used only on buttons. The tokens are already structured for that swap.
